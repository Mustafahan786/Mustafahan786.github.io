Real-Time sEMG Gesture Control for Upper-Limb Rehabilitation

Machine-learning gesture control and quantitative assessment of a video-game protocol for post-stroke hand rehabilitation.

Summary

Research question
Can sEMG-controlled video games support interactive hand rehabilitation after stroke, and how can task-performance metrics quantify their usability?
What I did
Developed and compared five classifiers, deployed gradient boosting for task control, and co-developed the interactive tasks and evaluation protocol.
Key result

Real-time task control was demonstrated, and Space Shooter completion times followed task difficulty, supporting graded assessment of user performance.

Fitts’ law fit: R2 = 0.99 for the able-bodied group; 0.88 for one stroke participant.

Overview

Surface electromyography (sEMG) measures muscle electrical activity using electrodes placed on the skin. This project developed an sEMG-driven video-game protocol for interactive post-stroke hand rehabilitation. Wrist and hand gestures recognised from forearm sEMG were translated into real-time commands for three games, and participants adjusted their gestures using visual feedback from the game.

The evaluation protocol assessed user performance across repeated sessions through target acquisition and maze navigation. Fitts’ law related target-acquisition completion time to task difficulty, while completion rate, overshoot, task score and path efficiency provided complementary measures of performance.

Signal processing

A Myo armband, positioned approximately 5 cm from the elbow, recorded raw forearm sEMG and transmitted it over Bluetooth Low Energy to the acquisition software. The eight channels were sampled at 200 Hz per channel, z-score standardised and segmented into 150 ms windows with 73% overlap; four time-domain features per channel formed a 32-element feature vector. The gesture classifier assigned each feature vector to one of six classes: wrist flexion, wrist extension, radial deviation, ulnar deviation, hand closing or rest.

Gesture classification

Model Development and Selection

Five candidate classifiers—support vector machine, decision tree, XGBoost, CatBoost and gradient boosting—were implemented and tuned in Python. Offline cross-validation on labelled sEMG features from able-bodied participants informed model selection. Gradient boosting was selected for real-time gesture recognition.

Online Deployment

At the start of each session, the selected model was trained on the participant’s calibration recordings. During gameplay, the fixed model classified incoming feature vectors, and the predicted gesture labels were mapped to task commands.

Interactive tasks

Three task interfaces, developed in Pygame to resemble therapy exercises, converted the recognised gestures into game actions. Wrist flexion and extension controlled horizontal movement, radial and ulnar deviation controlled vertical movement, and hand closing triggered a discrete action. Brick Breaker used horizontal movement only; Space Shooter added the hand-close action; Maze combined both movement axes with the hand-close action.

In the figure, Brick Breaker appears under the name of the game it imitates, Arkanoid, and the hand-close action is labelled “Energy Recharge”: closing the hand filled an energy bar, which fired at the enemy ships in Space Shooter and broke the wooden blocks guarding the key in Maze. The horizontal mapping was mirrored for left-handed participants. Because each task added an independent control channel, the project refers to them as one-, two- and three-degree-of-freedom (DOF) tasks.

Evaluation protocol

Ten able-bodied male participants, two of them left-handed, and two male participants in the chronic phase after haemorrhagic stroke with right-sided hemiplegia took part. Each participant completed real-time sessions on five working days, with three sessions per day. Each session began with a calibration recording of the six classes, five repetitions of 5 s each, followed by model training and task performance. Brick Breaker and Space Shooter presented ten targets per session.

Tasks were matched to hand function. Stroke participant A performed all three tasks. Stroke participant B, who had more limited active wrist movement, performed Brick Breaker only, first using hand opening and closing and then wrist flexion and extension.

Target Acquisition and Fitts’ Law

To assess control within the rehabilitation games, target acquisition was evaluated at predefined levels of difficulty. In Brick Breaker and Space Shooter, the avatar returned to a fixed starting position before each trial, and target distance and width determined the index of difficulty.

A trial was completed only when the avatar remained inside the target until the trial ended. In Brick Breaker, the paddle had to stay in the target until the ball arrived; in Space Shooter, the ship had to stay in the target while hand closing filled the energy bar. Completion time therefore includes this hold condition, not movement time alone. The analysis evaluated a linear relationship between completion time and index of difficulty:

Results

Completion Time and Index of Difficulty

The Fitts’ law analysis evaluated Space Shooter, the target-acquisition task that combined horizontal movement with a hand-close action. Completion time was averaged for each of the ten target conditions, and a straight line was fitted to these means against index of difficulty. Mean completion time increased with index of difficulty: from about 0.7 to 3.3 s for the ten able-bodied participants (R2 = 0.99), and from about 1.1 to 4.0 s for stroke participant A, the one stroke participant who completed this task (R2 = 0.88).

Because each fit uses condition means, R2 describes how consistently average completion time followed task difficulty; the error bars show substantial variation between trials. The relationship between completion time and task difficulty supports the use of graded game tasks for quantitative assessment within the proposed rehabilitation protocol.

Task Performance Across Sessions

Participant A’s performance on day 1 and day 5 was compared using three sessions from each day.

No statistically significant change was detected in Space Shooter completion time or Maze path efficiency. Participant B showed no statistically significant difference in Brick Breaker.

Dataset and outputs

Dataset · 2023

MUSED-I: Multi-Gesture Surface Electromyography (sEMG) Dataset for Stroke Rehabilitation

M. M. Khan, M. Farhan, A. Shahzad, H. S. Qarni, A. Waris and O. Gillani

IEEE DataPort · 5 August 2023 · DOI 10.21227/04zq-yz45

Eight-channel Myo recordings of six classes (wrist flexion, wrist extension, hand closing, radial deviation, ulnar deviation and rest) from ten able-bodied participants and two participants after stroke, recorded in three daily sessions of five 5 s repetitions per gesture. The second stroke participant recorded three of the six classes.

  • IEEE DataPort record
  • GitHub repository

The project was carried out in the Human System Lab, School of Mechanical and Manufacturing Engineering, NUST, with faculty co-authors Asim Waris and Omer Gilani. My contribution covered sEMG signal acquisition and feature extraction, development and selection of the machine-learning models, and deployment of gradient boosting for real-time gesture recognition; the task interfaces and evaluation protocol were developed jointly with the co-authors. I am first author of the MUSED-I dataset.

Supported in part by NUST research grant NUST-22-41-48. The recording protocol was approved by the NUST Research Ethics Committee (M-20221206). Recordings were made under the supervision of physiotherapists and doctors at Mayo Hospital, Lahore, and NUST.
